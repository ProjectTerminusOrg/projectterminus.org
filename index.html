01 The challenge

AI agents can now write most of the code for computational science. They can investigate papers, help with ideas and suggest new experiments, and doing all of this in a loop can be immensely powerful. It also raises real questions of AI safety. An agent that works on its own spends computing, handles sensitive data and runs code on real machines. Somebody has to set its limits, and somebody has to be able to check what it did.

Adopting such a platform therefore requires a great deal of trust, because whoever runs it becomes the steward of valuable data and of what the agents are allowed to do. They see research before it is published, and they hold everything that passes through the loop: the ideas, the failed attempts and the reasons for changing course. That is the best material there is for training ever more powerful research AI, and it would sit with whoever runs the platform.

So before researchers and institutions hand over their work, they need clear answers. Who can see my unpublished work? Can it be used to train a model? Who decides what the agents may do on their own? Who sets the rules, and how can they change? Can I leave and take my work with me?

02 The solution

Terminus is a proposal for a platform that must be trusted. It has two parts. The first is the software and infrastructure that automate the steps between an idea and a result. A researcher formulates an idea, a question or a problem and attaches the relevant data and papers. An AI agent processes the data, investigates the problem and writes the code for the experiments. The code and data are sent to a compute node, perhaps a university’s HPC cluster, where a job is submitted and runs in a container. The results return to the agent, which analyses them and reports back to the researcher with suggestions for the next round. The researcher can approve each run or let the agent work on its own, within limits set by the people responsible for the data and the computing. Every step goes on one record, so anyone with the right access can check what the agent did and why.

A first prototype does this today for small machine-learning experiments. It runs on an institution’s own GPU node, with each run in an Apptainer container and its results tracked in MLflow. For now the researcher approves every run, and submitting jobs to a shared HPC cluster through SLURM is the next step.

The second part is the organisation and governance needed to trust such a platform. Its job is to make sure the answers to those questions don’t depend on whoever happens to run it, including how far the agents may go on their own and who may train AI on the research. It would start with one nonprofit that partners with local institutions. Then a second, independent organisation would start in another country. The governance would grow out of the collaboration between institutions and countries.

04 The organisation

One possible shape is a nonprofit in each country, running Terminus on that country’s computing and shaped by the institutions and researchers that take part. A shared body would take on only what no single country can do alone, such as the code, common standards (among them the limits agents work within) and working across borders. The national organisations would govern it together.

Much is still open: how organisations join and leave, how decisions are made, and whether national nodes are the right shape at all. Those are for the first participants to settle, not for me to decide in advance.

05 Get involved

Today, Terminus is one prototype and one founder. There is no nonprofit, no national node and no charter yet, and nothing on this page binds anyone. Anyone is welcome to get in touch, whether you want to try the prototype, bring it to your institution, or talk about how a platform like this should be governed and kept safe.
