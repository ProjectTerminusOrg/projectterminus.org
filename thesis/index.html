Project Terminus

Founding Thesis

Jesper Dybkær Lauridsen · November 2026

Download the PDF

AI-assisted and autonomous research is here, and it raises a range of issues.

  • The researcher wants utility: useful tools and platforms that can connect their ideas, experience and data with compute resources, from workstations to supercomputers. They want to be able to efficiently share their work and collaborate with researchers across the globe. And they want to be certain that their work is respected, cited and not used against their intentions or outside their control.
  • The institutional administrator or politician wants to support research while upholding the law and protecting sensitive data and IP. They want to maintain sovereignty and prevent brain drain. And they want to make sure that there is a system of governance and control that they can stand behind.
  • The sysadmin and operator of an HPC centre wants to support research, but they are extremely reluctant to install new software on their premises if that software is commercial or proprietary, if they don’t understand it, or if it would take away agency and control over their expensive hardware.
  • AI safety researchers and institutes are fighting hard to underline the necessity of comprehensive observability, auditing, control and the ability for global response to adverse events involving AI, especially autonomous AI.
    “As humans become less involved in AI R&D, they could lose both the opportunities and expertise needed to identify and fix problems.” (Chan et al., 2026, p. 6)

Despite the overwhelming demand, no approach has yet adequately addressed all these needs.

  • Tech giants, frontier labs and Silicon Valley startups can deliver the utility, but they fail to deliver on most other factors.
  • A “CERN for AI” could have the ambition, but coordinating this would take too long when we need it now, and it would require an incredible feat of diplomatic will.
    “Legal, institutional, and physical safeguards can take years to establish and would come too late if preparations began only after such capabilities had already arrived. Many preparations therefore need to start now.” (Chan et al., 2026, p. 8)
  • Local institutions can develop solutions to some of these issues, namely practical utility for researchers while satisfying local administrators. Because they are integrated with local infrastructure, they have the trust and can be allowed to install capable software on, for example, university HPC premises.

Local solutions have difficulty attracting the talent and funding necessary to compete with VC-backed startups. But even if they have the talent and funding, they find themselves in a local optimum: The local integration that allows for a platform to exist locally is exactly what prevents it from gaining adoption globally, because you would need to convince an institution on the other side of the planet that they should join your platform and become second-class citizens to your success story, when they could just as well build their own platform to their specifications without ceding any local control. This, in turn, prevents the adoption of AI safety measures at global scale.

To reach the global optimum, one has to carefully balance neutrality with local integration. It should be possible for a local administration to feel and directly have ownership, while another administration in another country feels the exact same. One should have the ability to move fast and lightly shake things. Some barriers do need to be pushed. One should see a “sexy” organisation, an attractive place to work and a platform users would want to use regardless of who made it, stimulating a network effect.

Project Terminus is being built to address this. Technically, it involves a platform for AI-assisted and autonomous research, first within the domain of machine learning. The organisational vision is an expanding international federation of independent Terminus nodes. Each node is a nonprofit organisation and can enter into partnerships with institutions, HPC centres, industry and the government of a country. A local board would oversee and control the operation of the platform within its sphere of influence. Representatives from each node would govern international affairs.

With inspiration from intergovernmental organisations like CERN, and software initiatives like Linux/The Linux Foundation, Project Terminus will tackle the issue of building the complex, federated governance necessary for comprehensive AI observability and control. But it will not do so from the top, like CERN, or from the bottom, like a university project. It will do so from the middle, launching with nodes in 2–3 different countries, which can expand both locally, becoming integrated in the infrastructure of each respective country, and globally, when new nodes are created and decide to join.

My suggestion is to first start nodes in Montreal, Canada, in London, UK, and in Copenhagen, Denmark. Help me find founders in these locations, who are willing to build teams and create a global platform.
